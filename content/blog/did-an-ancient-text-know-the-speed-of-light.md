I remember I was around 14, sitting in the audience at an event, when I first heard someone on stage say that the speed of light was already mentioned in our scriptures. I was fascinated. I couldn't stop talking about it with my friends and family. To think that our ancestors knew something so advanced, so long ago.

Later, when I started studying physics, I began to grow sceptical. Not of our heritage, but of claims that were repeated without anyone checking them.

Don't get me wrong. When I question an idea like this, I don't mean that ancient India had nothing to offer. I study Indian astronomy in my free time, read about the discoveries we can support with evidence, and have attended online lectures on the subject. One of my future plans is to study and explore ancient Indian astronomy more deeply and make documentaries about it.

But as a sceptic, I also want to be extra careful that claims which could be wrong or misleading don't spread in our culture's name.

So when a peer-reviewed paper on exactly this question came out in August 2026, I wanted to know two things. What does the ancient statement actually say? And does the claim survive a real physics test?

The paper is by Kartik Iyer, Mohit Gour and Govind Maurya, published in the *Proceedings of the Indian National Science Academy*. I checked its calculations, then I tested the claim against the astronomy of the same traditions it draws its units from.

## What the Paper Actually Says

The statement comes from Sayana, a 14th-century scholar who wrote a famous commentary on the Rigveda. While explaining a hymn to the Sun, he quoted a traditional line: the Sun travels 2,202 yojanas in half a nimesa.

A **yojana** is an old unit of distance. A **nimesa** is a very short unit of time, often described as the blink of an eye.

![The line from Sayana's commentary as printed in the paper, with the translation and the note that it is not present in the Rigveda highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig01_sayana_quote.webp)

*Figure 1: The line quoted in Sayana's commentary, and the paper's note that it is not part of the Rigveda itself. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 3)*

The paper gets one important point right. This number is not a verse of the Rigveda. It appears in Sayana's commentary, quoted as something traditionally remembered.

The authors collect different values for the yojana and the nimesa from old texts, combine them into 16 possible speeds, and compare each with the speed of light in vacuum, exactly 299,792.458 km/s.

Their final paragraph explicitly disclaims an experimentally established ancient measurement.

![The paper's final paragraph, with its statement that it does not claim a definitive or experimentally established determination of the speed of light highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig14_final_limitation.webp)

*Figure 2: The paper's own limitation, in its final paragraph. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 12)*

But elsewhere the paper speaks more confidently. Its abstract refers to "Sāyaṇācārya's numerical value for the speed of light", and its conclusion says he "postulates" one (Figure 32). So the paper doesn't claim an ancient experiment, but it does identify this number with the speed of light. That identification is what I tested.

## How the Calculation Works

"2,202 yojanas in half a nimesa" is a speed. To turn it into kilometres per second, we need two conversions:

- **Y** = how many kilometres are in one yojana
- **N** = how many seconds are in one nimesa

The distance is 2,202 × Y kilometres. The time is half a nimesa, or N ÷ 2 seconds. Speed is distance divided by time:

**Speed = 2,202 × Y ÷ (N ÷ 2) = 4,404 × Y ÷ N** (km/s)

The 4,404 is just 2 × 2,202. It already takes care of the "half", so you divide by the full nimesa.

So the answer depends entirely on which conversions you choose. A bigger yojana gives a bigger speed; a longer nimesa gives a smaller one.

To compare each answer with the real speed of light, the paper uses a **percentage difference**: how far the answer is above or below the real value, as a share of that value. +6% means 6% too high. −15% means 15% too low.

## Turning a Yojana into Kilometres

For several of its astronomical values, the paper finds the length of a yojana using **calibration**: checking an unknown measuring scale against something whose size you already know.

Imagine you find an old ruler with markings you don't recognise. You know the table it was used to measure is 2 metres long, and the old ruler says the table is 10 units. Then each unit must be about 20 cm.

The paper does the same with Earth. Old astronomy texts give Earth's size in yojanas. Compare that with Earth's modern size, and you get kilometres per yojana:

**km per yojana = modern Earth size in km ÷ historical Earth size in yojanas**

![The paper's calibration method, with its stated Earth radius of 6,437 km highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig03_earth_radius_method.webp)

*Figure 3: The calibration method. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 5)*

This is a reasonable starting point, but it is conditional:

- It gives the kilometre value a text's yojana would need for its stated Earth size to match the modern one. If the text's Earth size was off, that error is silently absorbed into the yojana.
- It doesn't tell us which yojana Sayana's line had in mind.
- A ruler set using Earth will, of course, get Earth right. That part is not an independent check.

Keep that last point in mind for the pressure test.

## The Arithmetic Holds Up

I recalculated all 16 speeds in the paper's Tables 3 to 5, using the printed inputs. Every one reproduces, to the displayed rounding. The full list is in the appendix.

![Table 3 of the paper, showing twelve speed calculations](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig07_table3.webp)

*Figure 4: Twelve of the 16 tabulated speeds. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 7, Table 3)*

![Tables 4 and 5 of the paper, with the Manusmriti result and the Table 5 inputs and results highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig08_tables4_5.webp)

*Figure 5: The other four, including the closest of the 16, the Manusmriti row. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 8, Tables 4 and 5)*

Among these 16 results, the closest is the row labelled Manusmriti. It uses a yojana of 12.87 km and a nimesa of 0.1778 s:

**4,404 × 12.87 ÷ 0.1778 ≈ 318,782.227 km/s**, about **6.334% above** the modern value.

The division is fine. What I wanted to know is whether the number agrees with the astronomy in the same texts.

## The Physics Pressure Test

The paper treats the line as a speed and converts it. But a number taken from an astronomical tradition should also make sense inside that tradition's own astronomy. So here is a test of the line inside the very texts the paper draws its units from. I use only nimesa values the paper itself pairs with each tradition.

One note first. Every astronomy before the telescope, Greek astronomy included, placed the Sun far too close. This is not a criticism of ancient astronomers. It's a test of the claim that this line encodes the speed of light.

### Test 1: Why the Earth-calibrated rows look good

Aryabhata gives Earth's diameter as 1,050 yojanas. With the paper's 12.13 km yojana, Earth's equator spins at about 0.464 km/s (3.1416 × 1,050 × 12.13 km ÷ 86,164 s). The real value, from NASA's equatorial radius of 6,378.137 km, is 0.465 km/s.

![The Aryabhata rows of the paper's Table 1, with Earth's diameter of 1,050 yojanas and the 12.13 km yojana](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig04_table1_aryabhata_rows.webp)

*Figure 6: Aryabhata's Earth diameter and the paper's yojana. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 5, Table 1)*

![NASA Earth Fact Sheet with the equatorial radius 6,378.137 km and mean radius 6,371 km highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_nasa_radius.webp)

*Figure 7: Earth's radius. (NASA NSSDC, [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html), accessed 25 October 2026)*

A near-perfect match, and it has to be: the yojana was set using Earth's size and the nimesa using the length of the day. For every row where the yojana comes from Earth's size, agreement near Earth is built in. It can't independently confirm the conversion. (The paper's two closest rows take other routes, Jha's 8 miles and an archaeological reading. Those are examined in the next section.)

### Test 2: Read literally, the line contradicts its own astronomy

The line addresses the Sun: you traverse 2,202 yojanas in half a nimesa. The paper itself notes that a Sun's-speed reading isn't supported by the astronomical texts.

![The paper's report that calculating the Sun's speed with such a formula is not supported by Siddhantic or Puranic texts, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig19_sun_speed_kak.webp)

*Figure 8: (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 9)*

The numbers show how far off it is. Aryabhata counts 4,320,000 solar revolutions and 1,577,917,500 days in a yuga, so his year is 1,577,917,500 ÷ 4,320,000 = 365.2587 days. His Sun's orbit is 2,887,666.8 yojanas (shown in Test 3 below), so the Sun moves along it about 2,887,666.8 ÷ 365.2587 ≈ 7,906 yojanas per day. Sayana's rate, with the paper's Vishnu Purana count of 405,000 nimesas per day, is about 1.78 billion yojanas per day: **more than 200,000 times faster**. Even the Sun's apparent daily trip around the whole sky, 2.89 million yojanas, is more than 600 times too slow.

![Wikipedia's Kali ahargana article listing the Aryabhatiya's 4,320,000 solar revolutions and 1,577,917,500 civil days per yuga, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_wiki_days.webp)

*Figure 9: Aryabhata's yuga constants. (Wikipedia, [Kali ahargana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali_ahargana), accessed 25 October 2026)*

![The paper's Table 2, with the Vishnu Purana's 405,000 nimesas per day](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig06_table2_sidereal.webp)

*Figure 10: The Vishnu Purana's 405,000 nimesas per day, used for Sayana's daily rate: 4,404 × 405,000 = 1,783,620,000 yojanas per day. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 7, Table 2)*

Both sides of this comparison are in the tradition's own yojanas, so no conversion choice can change the result. As written, the line doesn't describe anything in this astronomy. To get a speed-of-light reading, it has to be reinterpreted, which is what the paper does.

### Test 3: Read as light, it doesn't fit the sky it came with

So take the paper's reading. The same texts that give Earth's size also give the Sun's distance, in the same yojanas. If 2,202 yojanas per half nimesa is the speed of light, how long does sunlight take to reach Earth inside each tradition's own model? The real answer is about 8 minutes 19 seconds: 149,598,000 km ÷ 299,792.458 km/s ≈ 499 seconds. In Earth radii, the real distance is 149,598,000 ÷ 6,371 ≈ 23,481.

Here is the result for all three traditions. The working for each follows the table.

**Table A: Sayana's line read as light, inside each tradition's own sky.**

| Tradition | Sun's distance in the text | Nimesa (paper's pairing) | Sunlight would arrive in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aryabhata | 459,585 yojanas | 0.2133 s | 22.3 s |
| Surya Siddhanta | 684,870 yojanas | 0.088 s | 13.7 s |
| Vishnu Purana | 100,000 yojanas | 0.2133 s | 4.8 s |
| Reality | 149.6 million km | | 499 s |

<div class="callout">

**What this shows, and what it doesn't.** In each tradition, three things can't all be right at once: its Sun distance, its yojana, and Sayana's line as the speed of light. We already know the Sun distances are wrong, so this test alone can't tell us the speed is wrong.

But it does show two things. First, the number can't have come from these astronomies, or been checked against them: inside their own skies, a speed near c would put sunlight here in seconds. Second, the "match" exists only when you take a unit from one source and leave out the sky that came with it. That step is made by the modern interpreter, not by the texts.

</div>

**The working, tradition by tradition.**

![NASA Earth Fact Sheet with the semimajor axis of 149.598 million km highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_nasa_axis.webp)

*Figure 11: The real Earth to Sun distance. (NASA NSSDC, [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html), accessed 25 October 2026)*

If the Sun is D yojanas away, light at 4,404 yojanas per nimesa arrives in:

**Light time = (D ÷ 4,404) × N seconds**

The yojana cancels out, so no kilometre conversion affects this result.

![The paper's description of Aryabhata's yojana, with the Sun's orbit of 2,887,666.8 yojanas and the orbit of the sky highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig02_aryabhata_orbits.webp)

*Figure 12: Aryabhata's Sun orbit, printed in the paper itself. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 4)*

**Aryabhata.** The paper prints the Sun's orbit as 2,887,666.8 yojanas around, which puts the Sun about 2,887,666.8 ÷ (2 × 3.1416) ≈ 459,585 yojanas away. With Earth's radius at 525 yojanas, that's about **875 Earth radii**, against a real 23,481, so about 27 times too close. With the paper's 0.2133 s nimesa: 459,585 ÷ 4,404 × 0.2133 = **22.3 seconds**. (Aryabhata's Moon, by contrast, sits about 65.5 Earth radii away against a real 60.3. The model works near Earth and breaks further out.)

**Surya Siddhanta.** Earth's diameter is 1,600 yojanas (verse I.59) and the Sun's orbit 4,331,500 yojanas (verse XII.86). The text gives its own rule for distance (verse XII.84): multiply the orbit by Earth's diameter and divide by Earth's circumference to get the orbit's diameter. With Earth's circumference at 1,600 × √10 ≈ 5,059.6 yojanas, the orbit's diameter is 4,331,500 × 1,600 ÷ 5,059.6 ≈ 1,369,741 yojanas. That puts the Sun about 684,870 yojanas from Earth's centre, or 684,870 ÷ 800 ≈ **856 Earth radii**. With the paper's 0.088 s nimesa: 684,870 ÷ 4,404 × 0.088 = **13.7 seconds**.

*Figure 13: Surya Siddhanta I.59. (Burgess 1935, printed p. 43; [PDF](https://classicalastrologer.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/surya_siddhanta_english.pdf) page 96, accessed 25 October 2026)*

![Burgess's translation, printed page 294, verses 84 to 86, with the distance rule and the Sun's orbit of 4,331,500 yojanas](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_burgess_p294.webp)

*Figure 14: Surya Siddhanta XII.84 to 86: the text's own distance rule, and the Sun's orbit. (Burgess 1935, printed p. 294; PDF page 347)*

![Burgess's table of orbits in yojanas, printed page 295, with the Sun at 4,331,500](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_burgess_p295.webp)

*Figure 15: Burgess's table of the orbits in verses XII.85 to 90. (Burgess 1935, printed p. 295; PDF page 348)*

**Vishnu Purana.** The Vishnu Purana places the Sun 100,000 yojanas from Earth and the Moon a further 100,000 beyond it. (Wilson's translation renders yojanas as "leagues".)

![Wilson's translation of the Vishnu Purana, Book II, Chapter VII, page 212, with the solar orb a hundred thousand leagues from the earth highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_vp.webp)

*Figure 16: Vishnu Purana II.7. Wilson's footnote adds that the Padma, Kurma and Vayu Puranas give a similar account. I use it because the paper takes its main nimesa from this text, and links Sayana's source to this broad tradition.

![The paper's statement that the source quoted by Sayana was most probably based on the Puranic-Dharmashastric tradition, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig21_puranic_dharmasastric_source.webp)

With the Vishnu Purana's own nimesa (0.2133 s): 100,000 ÷ 4,404 × 0.2133 = **4.8 seconds**.

### Test 4: Is there any evidence of a measurement?

The paper disclaims a measurement, but the popular claim usually implies one. Historically, there have been two routes to measuring the speed of light.

On the ground, you have to time light over a distance of kilometres, where it takes only tens of millionths of a second. Galileo proposed a lantern experiment in 1638; when the Accademia del Cimento in Florence tried it in 1667, with lanterns about a mile apart, no delay was observed. The first successful measurement on the ground was Fizeau's in 1849, using a rapidly rotating toothed wheel and a mirror about 8 km away.

In the sky, Ole Rømer made the first estimate in 1676 by timing the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io, and Christiaan Huygens turned it into a speed by combining it with an estimate of the size of Earth's orbit. That method only works if you know that size. These traditions placed the Sun 27 or more times too close, so an estimate built on their own scale would have come out roughly that many times too small.

![Wikipedia's account of the first attempts to measure light's speed, with no delay was observed and Huygens combining Rømer's estimate with the diameter of Earth's orbit highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_wiki_attempts.webp)

*Figure 18: First measurement attempts. (Wikipedia, [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), accessed 25 October 2026)*

![Wikipedia's table of historical measurements of c, with the Rømer and Huygens row and the 1849 Fizeau row highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_wiki_table.webp)

*Figure 19: History of measurements of c. (Wikipedia, [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), accessed 25 October 2026)*

The paper cites no instrument or procedure for either route, and I know of none. That doesn't prove no measurement ever happened. It means there is no evidence one did, and the one route that existed in principle would have failed with these traditions' own distances.

So agreement with c, whether built in through Earth calibration or reached by choosing a unit, doesn't validate the conversion on its own.

## The Unit Choices Behind the Match

The pressure test shows the resemblance depends on the modern conversion. So how well supported are the conversions the paper actually uses? For each, here's what the paper says, what I checked, what it shows, and what it doesn't.

### 1. Two Arthashastra yojanas, two very different answers

**What the paper says.** Table 1 lists two yojana values connected to the Arthashastra, an ancient text on statecraft. One is 12.3 km, labelled "Arthashastra". The other is 15.23 km, labelled "Archaeological Studies (Dholavira)". Table 4's Arthashastra row uses 15.23 km.

![Table 1's two Arthashastra-related yojana values, 15.23 km and 12.3 km, and Table 4's Arthashastra row using 15.23 km, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v3_arthashastra_choice.webp)

*Figure 20: Two values in Table 1, and the one used in Table 4. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 5, Table 1 and p. 8, Table 4)*

**What I checked.** On page 4, the paper says a yojana is 8,000 dhanus, that a dhanus is 108 angulas, and then that a yojana is 768,000 angulas.

![Two passages from the same page: 8,000 dhanus of 108 angulas, and a yojana of 768,000 angulas, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig15_length_ladder.webp)

*Figure 21: (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 4)*

Those numbers don't fit. 8,000 × 108 = 864,000, not 768,000. The 768,000 works out to 96 angulas per dhanus, which matches the ordinary dhanus of the Arthashastra in Shamasastry's translation. There, the 108-angula dhanus is a separate measure used for roads and fort walls. So the paper mixes two different dhanus in one passage. Combined with its two angula sizes (1.763 cm and 1.6 cm), this reconstructs both rounded values. (The translation's main text gives 1,000 dhanus per goruta and 4 gorutas per yojana; its footnote records that the commentator reads 2,000 per goruta, which gives the paper's 8,000 dhanus.)

![Shamasastry's Arthashastra, Book 2, Chapter 20, with 12 angulas to a vitasti, 2 vitastis to an aratni, 4 aratnis to a dhanus, and 108 angulas to a garhapatya dhanus highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_arth_units.webp)

*Figure 22: Arthashastra II.20: 12 × 2 × 4 = 96 angulas to the ordinary dhanus; 108 to the garhapatya dhanus. (Shamasastry tr., [wisdomlib.org](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/kautilya-arthashastra/d/doc366066.html), accessed 25 October 2026)*

![The translator's footnotes, with 2,000 dhanus per goruta and the ordinary dhanus of 96 angulas highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_arth_notes.webp)

*Figure 23: Arthashastra II.20, footnotes 7 to 9. (Same source)*

Using the Arthashastra's own time unit of 0.24 s:

**Table B: The two Arthashastra-related yojanas, with the same 0.24 s nimesa.**

| Yojana in the paper | Reconstructed recipe | Speed | Difference from c |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15.23 km (used in Table 4) | 8,000 × 108 angulas of 1.763 cm | 279,470.5 km/s | about −6.8% |
| 12.3 km (labelled Arthashastra in Table 1) | 8,000 × 96 angulas of 1.6 cm | 225,705 km/s | about −24.7% |

The first row is the paper's published result. The second is my check of the value the paper itself labels Arthashastra. It is not among the paper's 16 tabulated speeds.

<div class="callout">

**What it shows.** One choice of units decides whether this row looks close to the speed of light or not. The paper needs to explain why the 108-angula archaeological value is the right one for Sayana's line.

**What it doesn't show.** The archaeological reading may have support, and the paper gives a reason for it on page 4. This doesn't show intent: the paper publishes results far above c and rejects an earlier, closer calculation (p. 10). But a better match can't, by itself, show a choice was historically correct.

</div>

### 2. Where the closest row's yojana comes from

**What the paper says.** The 12.87 km yojana in the closest row is credited to Ganganath Jha, writing on Manu 8.406, who equates one yojana with 8 miles.

![The paper's statement that Jha, on Manusmriti verse 8.406, says one yojana equals 8 miles, which gives 12.87 km, and the matching Table 1 row](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v2_jha_manu.webp)

*Figure 24: The source of the 12.87 km yojana. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 4 and p. 5, Table 1)*

**What I checked.** Manu 8.406 is about boat fares on long river journeys. In [Jha's English rendering](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-the-commentary-of-medhatithi/d/doc201341.html) of the commentary, "one Yojana (8 miles)" appears as a parenthetical English gloss. The [Sanskrit commentary](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manusmriti-with-manubhashya-sanskrit/d/doc1648181.html) at that point talks about one and two yojanas, but gives no conversion into miles or any other unit. You can check both yourself.

![Manu 8.406 in Jha's translation: for a long passage, the boat fare should be in proportion to the time and place](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_manu_en_verse.webp)

*Figure 25: Manu 8.406, Jha's English translation. (wisdomlib.org, accessed 25 October 2026)*

![Jha's English rendering of Medhatithi's commentary on Manu 8.406, with one Yojana (8 miles) highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_manu_en_8miles.webp)

*Figure 26: The "(8 miles)" gloss in Jha's English rendering of the commentary. (Same page)*

![Medhatithi's Sanskrit commentary on Manu 8.406, with every mention of yojana highlighted, none converted to miles](/images/blog/sayana-audit/ext_manu_sa.webp)

*Figure 27: The same passage in Sanskrit: one yojana or two, no miles. (wisdomlib.org, Manubhashya, accessed 25 October 2026)*

<div class="callout">

**What it shows.** The closest result in the paper rests on an English gloss. The cited passage, on its own, doesn't establish an ancient length unit, paired with an ancient time unit, that applies to Sayana's statement.

**What it doesn't show.** I'm not saying the 8-mile figure is wrong or unsupported elsewhere, and this says nothing against Manu's time units. The question is what else supports this conversion, and why it applies to Sayana's line.

</div>

### 3. An Earth size that doesn't match its cited source

**What the paper says.** For the Surya Siddhanta, the paper takes Earth's circumference as 4,800 yojanas, citing Burgess's English translation. From this it gets about 8.35 km per yojana.

![The paper's statement that in the Surya Siddhanta Earth's circumference is 4800 yojanas, giving 8.35 km per yojana, and the matching Table 1 row](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v2_ss_4800_passage.webp)

*Figure 28: (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 4 and p. 5, Table 1)*

**What I checked.** In Burgess's translation, verse I.59 (printed page 43, Figure 13) gives Earth's diameter as 1,600 yojanas, with a circumference rule of the square root of ten times the diameter squared: **1,600 × √10 ≈ 5,059.64 yojanas**, not 4,800. Burgess's next page gives 5,059.556. Venugopal and colleagues, cited by the paper itself, use this Burgess figure, and note that 4,800 appears in other works (2019, printed p. 403).

<div class="callout">

**What it shows.** The number used doesn't match the cited source, and it's the starting point of that calibration.

**What it doesn't show.** 4,800 is genuinely used in related traditions, so it wasn't made up. Using about 5,060 would actually move that row a little closer to c, though it would stay above.

</div>

### 4. Does a bigger unit make a better fit?

**What the paper says.** The paper sets aside the smaller yojana values as local units, saying astronomy needs "larger and more consistent units".

![The paper's statement that smaller yojana estimates are disregarded as unsuitable for astronomical contexts where larger units are required, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v2_smaller_yojanas.webp)

*Figure 29: (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, pp. 4 and 5)*

<div class="callout">

**What it shows.** Physics doesn't need a large unit to describe a large speed. The speed of light is the same in kilometres or millimetres per second. So the size of a unit, and its supposed suitability for astronomy, can't by themselves establish which yojana Sayana's quotation means.

**What it doesn't show.** A real local-versus-astronomical distinction could exist. It needs historical evidence about this quotation, not the size of the unit.

</div>

## Smaller Errors

These don't decide the question, but they should be corrected:

- **Two wrong entries in the paper's Table 2.** Dividing the paper's sidereal day (86,164.0905 s, the time Earth takes to spin once relative to the stars) by 486,000 gives 0.17729 s, not the printed 0.17740 s. Dividing it by 202,500 gives 0.42550 s, not 0.42584 s. Neither feeds the 16 speeds. All seven entries are checked in the appendix.
- **The 0.088 s nimesa.** The paper's text gives about 0.0886 s and its Table 2 gives 0.08865 s and 0.08889 s, all of which round to 0.089. Table 5 uses 0.088 (p. 6).
- **Which modern Earth?** The calibration uses a radius of "approximately 6,437 km". NASA gives 6,371 km (mean) and 6,378.137 km (equatorial). Different parts of the paper also imply different Earth sizes: Table 1's Nilakantha and Yallaya values imply about 6,439 km, while the same scholars' values in the prose on page 3 imply about 6,376 km.
- **"Within a 33% margin"** (p. 9) describes a pair the paper's own Table 5 puts at +34.7%, and credits it to Brahmagupta, while Tables 1 and 5 credit the 8.07 km yojana to Bhaskara II.
- On page 7, the yojanas are said to range up to 15.54 km; Table 1's largest is 15.23 km. The verse is cited as Rigveda 1.50.4 on page 2 and 1.50.5 on page 9. Page 4 says "800 angulas" for the 15.232 km yojana.

## How Strong Is the Argument?

**Many conversions are not many measurements.** The 16 speeds are 16 readings of one sentence, not 16 independent measurements of light. Several landing in the same range tells us about the units chosen, not about separate confirmations.

**A specific number is not a measured number.** The paper argues that 2,202 is oddly specific, as if someone was aiming for precision.

![The paper's argument that the specific figure of 2202 suggests an effort at precision rather than exaggeration, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v2_precision_2202.webp)

*Figure 30: (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, pp. 10 and 11)*

But writing 2,202 rather than 2,200 doesn't tell us the method, the uncertainty, or the accuracy behind it. The paper itself offers 2,200 as an example of a rounded figure, and the difference is only about 0.09%, far smaller than the spread between the paper's own unit choices, from about −16% to +39%.

**A possible reading becomes a firm one.** On page 9, the paper suggests the Sun's "speed" may be a figurative way of describing the speed of sunlight.

![The paper's suggestion that the Sun's speed may reasonably be taken as a figurative reference to the swiftness of sunlight, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/v2_sunlight_reading.webp)

*Figure 31: A possible reading, offered on page 9. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 9)*

By page 11, it describes Sayana as postulating a value for the speed of light.

![The paper's conclusion describing Sayana as postulating a numerical value for the speed of light, highlighted](/images/blog/sayana-audit/fig12_postulates.webp)

*Figure 32: Firmer wording on page 11. (Iyer, Gour & Maurya 2026, p. 11)*

Page 12 then disclaims an experimental determination (Figure 2). But a figurative reading can't become a postulated value without evidence, and the pressure test shows the sunlight reading gets no support from the traditions' own skies.

## So, Where Does This Leave Us?

When I heard this claim from that stage at 14, it felt thrilling. Testing it properly, years later, I understand why it's so convincing, and why it doesn't hold.

The paper's 16 divisions are correct, and with the right units the line really does land within about 6% of the speed of light.

But read as written, the line says the Sun moves over 200,000 times faster than its own astronomy allows. Read as light, it doesn't fit the skies of the traditions it comes from: inside them, sunlight would arrive in seconds. The resemblance to c appears only after a modern conversion, one that takes a unit from one source and leaves the rest of that tradition's sky behind.

And those conversions are thin. The closest result rests on an English gloss. One Arthashastra row swings from about 7% to about 25% off depending on which of the paper's own values you use. A key Earth size doesn't match its cited source. And there is no evidence of any instrument or method that could have measured the speed of light.

Put together, nothing here requires more than a coincidence of conversion, which is one of the explanations the paper itself keeps open. Agreement with c, on its own, doesn't validate the conversion or show that anyone knew the speed of light.

Indian astronomy has achievements that don't need rescuing, like Aryabhata's rotating Earth or Madhava's infinite series for sine. This line isn't one of them. The paper itself stops short of claiming a measurement. The popular version of this claim should stop there too.

## Check It Yourself

Everything below works on an ordinary calculator.

<details class="fold">
<summary>Show the formulas</summary>

**Speed from any row:** `4404 × Y ÷ N`. Use the full nimesa, because the 4,404 already includes the "half".

**Percentage difference:** `100 × (speed ÷ 299792.458 − 1)`. Positive means above the speed of light, negative means below.

**Sunlight travel time in a tradition's own units:** `(D ÷ 4404) × N` seconds, where D is the Sun's distance in yojanas. For an orbit given as a circumference C, `D = C ÷ (2 × π)`.

- Aryabhata: `2887666.8 ÷ (2 × π) ÷ 4404 × 0.2133 ≈ 22.3` s
- Surya Siddhanta, using its own rule (XII.84) with circumference = √10 × diameter: `4331500 ÷ √10 ÷ 2 ÷ 4404 × 0.088 ≈ 13.7` s
- Vishnu Purana: `100000 ÷ 4404 × 0.2133 ≈ 4.8` s
- Reality: `149597870.7 ÷ 299792.458 ≈ 499` s

**The line read literally, in Aryabhata's model:** the Sun's daily motion is `2887666.8 ÷ 365.2587 ≈ 7906` yojanas per day, while Sayana's rate is `4404 × 405000 ≈ 1.78 billion` yojanas per day, over 200,000 times more. (365.2587 is Aryabhata's year: 1,577,917,500 days ÷ 4,320,000 revolutions.)

**Sun's distance in Earth radii:** `(C ÷ (2 × π)) ÷ (Earth diameter in yojanas ÷ 2)`. Aryabhata: `459585 ÷ 525 ≈ 875`. Surya Siddhanta: `684870 ÷ 800 ≈ 856`. Reality: `149597870.7 ÷ 6371 ≈ 23481`.

**The Surya Siddhanta Sun orbit, from its own rules:** the Moon's orbit of 324,000 yojanas, times the Moon's 57,753,336 revolutions, divided by the Sun's 4,320,000 revolutions per great age: `324000 × 57753336 ÷ 4320000 ≈ 4331500`.

**The closest row:** `4404 × 12.87 ÷ 0.1778 = 318,782.227` km/s, about +6.334%.

**The two Arthashastra yojanas:** `4404 × 15.23 ÷ 0.24 = 279,470.5` km/s (about −6.78%), and `4404 × 12.3 ÷ 0.24 = 225,705` km/s (about −24.71%).

**The Surya Siddhanta circumference:** `1600 × √10 ≈ 5059.64` yojanas (the paper uses 4,800).
